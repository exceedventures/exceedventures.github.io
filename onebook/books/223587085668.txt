The Korean term dojagi (도자기) combines two words: dogi (도기), meaning pottery, and jagi (자기), meaning porcelain.
Pottery fired at around 800–1,000°C is called dogi, while ceramics fired at 1,300–1,500°C are called jagi, or porcelain. Together, they are referred to as dojagi.
Ceramics fired below 800°C are called earthenware, while those fired at 1,000–1,300°C are known as stoneware.
In 16th-century Europe, ceramic technology was still far behind that of East Asia.
Europe could produce earthenware, pottery, and stoneware, but Europeans had not yet developed kilns capable of withstanding temperatures above 1,300°C. As a result, they could not produce the hard, high-fired porcelain that was being made in East Asia.
In the 16th century, only two countries in the world were capable of producing porcelain:
Joseon Korea and China.
One type of porcelain that became extremely popular in Europe at the time was blue-and-white porcelain.
The distinctive blue color was created using cobalt pigment, known as hoehwacheong (回回靑). Cobalt was essentially the only blue pigment that could withstand firing temperatures above 1,300°C. Once cobalt was introduced, the production of blue-and-white porcelain became possible.
Cobalt was discovered in Persia and the Arabian Peninsula and was brought to Joseon through China.
Because it had to be imported through Persia and China, cobalt was an extremely expensive material.
After King Sejo saw blue-and-white porcelain imported from the Ming Dynasty, he issued a nationwide decree ordering Joseon to develop its own blue-and-white porcelain.
Eventually, cobalt deposits were discovered in Gangjin and Suncheon, allowing Joseon to produce its own domestic blue pigment, known as to-cheong.
In 1464, Joseon succeeded in producing blue-and-white porcelain.
However, Joseon porcelain was primarily produced by government-run kilns and consumed domestically by the yangban aristocracy. As a result, it was Chinese blue-and-white porcelain that made its way into Europe.
Chinese blue-and-white porcelain became enormously popular among European aristocrats. At one point, a single piece could fetch a price comparable to that of a good house.
Then came an unexpected connection between Joseon and Europe.
A 22-year-old Dutch East India Company employee named Hendrick Hamel was caught in a storm and shipwrecked on Jeju Island along with 36 fellow crew members.
Hamel remained detained in Joseon for 13 years. In September 1666, he escaped to Japan by ship, and in July 1668, he finally returned to the Netherlands.
After rejoining the Dutch East India Company, Hamel argued that because he had been shipwrecked while carrying out his company duties, his time in Joseon should be recognized as continuous employment.
In order to claim the wages he had accumulated during those years, Hamel organized his experiences and information about Joseon into a detailed report and submitted it to the company.
This became known as Hamel’s Journal of His Shipwreck in Jeju.
Based on this record, Hamel was able to receive 14 years’ worth of unpaid wages all at once.
The report he submitted to the Dutch East India Company eventually leaked to the public and was published under the title An Unfortunate Voyage of the Sperwer.
The book became known as Hamel’s Journal or Hamel’s Drift, and it became a bestseller in Europe.
First published in the Netherlands in 1668, the book was subsequently translated into French, German, and English, introducing Joseon Korea to a much wider European audience.
Through Hamel’s report, the Dutch East India Company learned that Joseon was also a country capable of producing blue-and-white porcelain.
In 1669, the company renamed a large 1,000-ton merchant ship that was under construction Korea, signaling the beginning of preparations for direct trade with Joseon.
The Korea departed Vlissingen on May 20, 1669, and began its voyage toward Joseon. It eventually reached Indonesia.
At the time, Japan was making enormous profits by selling cotton cloth, pepper, and other goods imported from the Dutch East India Company to Joseon. The profits were said to reach dozens or even hundreds of times the original purchase price.
The Japanese shogunate was concerned that these profits would disappear if the Dutch East India Company began trading directly with Joseon.
Japan therefore warned the Dutch East India Company that if it established direct trade with Joseon, Japan would close the Dutch trading post in Japan.
Meanwhile, in Arita, Saga Prefecture, Kyushu, Japan, descendants of Korean potter Yi Sam-pyeong, who had been taken to Japan during the Imjin War, had been producing porcelain since 1616.
Japan presented Arita porcelain as an alternative to Korean blue-and-white porcelain.
Arita porcelain quickly began selling extremely well in Europe, and its quality far surpassed that of the relatively simple blue Chinese porcelain that had previously dominated the market.
Over a period of 70 years, approximately seven million pieces of Arita porcelain were exported to Europe. By the 19th century, Japan had exported roughly 20 million pieces of ceramics to Europe.
Arita porcelain was also the origin of the teacup saucer and the teapot as we know them today.
The tea sets that helped establish the aristocratic European tea culture known as tea time were, in large part, inspired by Arita porcelain.
As for the Korea, it spent ten years waiting in Indonesia.
In the end, it never reached Joseon.
On November 15, 1679, the ship was finally decommissioned and scrapped.
One-line comment
History shows us that many things are surprisingly similar to what we see today.
It may be tempting to view Japan’s actions as simply spiteful or underhanded, but from another perspective, Japan may have been acting rationally in its own national interest.
Even if the Korea had reached Joseon, the country was not internally prepared to embrace international trade at the time.
To seize a major opportunity, it takes more than individuals and companies. Proper and effective support from the government is also essential.
