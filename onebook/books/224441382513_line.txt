Can SpaceX Succeed? (Feat. Entering the Mobile Market)
On October 8, 2026, Elon Musk's SpaceX agreed to buy the 800 MHz spectrum for $8 billion. Elon Musk boasted that this was "the final puzzle piece needed to ensure cell phones work everywhere across the United States." Shares of AT&T, Verizon, and T-Mobile each dropped around 6%, as the market interpreted the move as SpaceX becoming a genuine telecom operator.
To understand why this is a big deal, you first need to understand radio spectrum. Electromagnetic waves are invisible, but they ripple and spread outward like waves created when throwing a stone into a lake. The number of times a wave ripples per second is called frequency, measured in Hertz (Hz). 1 Hz means one oscillation per second, 1 MHz is 1 million, and 1 GHz is 1 billion. It is important to understand the significance of SpaceX acquiring the 800 MHz band this time, following its previous acquisition of the near-2 GHz band from EchoStar.
Think of frequency like a radio. When you turn a radio dial, different broadcasts play at different numbers. These numbers are frequencies—exclusive addresses granted by the government. In Korea, 107.7 MHz broadcasts SBS’s 'Cultwo Show', while 95.9 MHz is home to MBC’s 'Starry Night'. If MBC were to broadcast on 107.7 MHz, MBC's signal and SBS's show would mix together into static, making both unlistenable. The government issues licenses specifying that only a particular company can use a specific frequency number. Mobile phones operate on the exact same principle. Usable spectrum is limited and mostly secured by existing telecom carriers, which is why it is so difficult for new players to jump into the telecom business. (Note: These radio frequencies are based on the Seoul metropolitan area; in other regions like Busan, the same show is often broadcast on different frequencies because distant areas don't overlap, preventing interference. This is why spectrum licenses are divided by region.)
The 800 MHz spectrum bought by SpaceX isn't uniformly available across the U.S. either; its bandwidth varies by county from 4.85 MHz to 14 MHz.
The reason radios sound clear in rural areas beyond mountains is that lower frequencies travel farther and penetrate walls more easily. If you listen to noise from the apartment above, high-pitched yelling is hard to hear, but low-pitched thumps often carry through. Similarly, radio waves with slower oscillations and longer wavelengths (about 37 cm for 800 MHz versus about 15 cm for 2 GHz) pass through obstacles like walls and buildings better and travel longer distances. While lower frequencies penetrate obstacles well and go far, their downside is carrying less data. Compared to 800 MHz, the strength of 2 GHz is its ability to carry a large volume of data at once. In other words, compared to SpaceX’s existing 2 GHz spectrum, the newly acquired 800 MHz travels relatively farther and penetrates inside walls better. Combining these two frequencies yields great results, which is why telecom carriers mix low and high bands—using low bands to cover broad areas and indoor spaces, and high bands to boost speeds in crowded locations.
Confusion often arises because the same unit (like MHz) is used to denote both position and bandwidth. The spectrum SpaceX bought consists of two blocks: 817–824 MHz and 862–869 MHz. Saying they bought 800 MHz means the frequency sits near the 800 mark on the dial. Saying they bought 14 MHz refers to the width (bandwidth) of the land. 817–824 MHz is 7 MHz, and 862–869 MHz is 7 MHz, totaling 14 MHz. Thus, "14 MHz in the 800 MHz band" means buying a "14-unit plot of land near mark #800."
When T-Mobile remarked, "We have 400 MHz while SpaceX only has around 80 MHz," they were talking about the area of this land. The wider the bandwidth, the more data can be transmitted at once. SpaceX's 2 GHz land is an 80 MHz wide highway, while this 800 MHz acquisition is a 14 MHz two-lane local road. SpaceX bought the two blocks as a pair because mobile frequencies naturally operate in pairs: signals must travel from the phone to the cell tower and from the cell tower back to the phone, requiring a two-way road.
In major cities, cell towers are usually located a few hundred meters to a few kilometers away, whereas direct-to-cell satellites orbit hundreds of kilometers above Earth. Because radio signals weaken over distance, sending signals directly from satellites to cell phones has been a challenging technology. Small antennas in smartphones cannot easily pick up weak signals, which is why satellite services have been limited to text messaging and basic data. It is also why using Starlink internet requires installing a pizza-box-sized dish outdoors.
The 2 GHz spectrum SpaceX bought from EchoStar is a high band that offers fast speeds but struggles to penetrate buildings. Until now, SpaceX's mobile phone service played a "supplementary" role, borrowing T-Mobile's spectrum to send texts in remote mountain areas or oceans where cell towers do not exist.
SpaceX's past four years tell the story of a tenant buying their own commercial property to compete with their landlord. In 2022, SpaceX partnered with T-Mobile. SpaceX launched satellites into the sky, and those satellites used T-Mobile’s spectrum to beam signals directly to T-Mobile customers' phones. This allowed basic texting in areas without cell towers, such as deep wilderness or open sea, and officially launched in July 2025 under the name 'T-Satellite'. At that time, SpaceX was a subcontractor working on T-Mobile’s land. SpaceX ran the operations, but T-Mobile owned both the land and the customers.
In September and November 2025, SpaceX decided to purchase near-2 GHz spectrum from satellite TV provider EchoStar for a total of $19.6 billion. After acquiring the 2 GHz spectrum, SpaceX began making statements about "definitely building ground networks," openly challenging incumbent carriers. The carriers did not sit idly by. In May 2026, long-time rivals AT&T, Verizon, and T-Mobile announced a joint agreement to operate a shared satellite service in response.
SpaceX currently operates three main business lines: Starlink, AI, and rocket launches. Looking at Q2 2026 results, Starlink generated $1.7 billion in operating profit, while AI lost $1.3 billion and rocket launches lost $500 million. However, profit and cash flow must be viewed separately. In Q2, despite Starlink's $1.7 billion profit, SpaceX spent $15.8 billion on AI data centers alone. For the entire first half of the year, the company earned about $3.5 billion in cash from operations but spent about $28.5 billion on capital expenditures. The shortfall is being covered by corporate bonds and funds raised from its June IPO. While Starlink makes money, it acts less like an endless bankroll and more like a guarantor that enables the company to borrow capital. In the eyes of banks and bond investors, Starlink is a "booming national franchise" that guarantees the company can pay back its debts. If Starlink stumbles, the financial standing of the entire group shakes; Starlink must perform well for SpaceX to borrow money for AI investments. Finding Starlink's next growth engine has become a matter of survival for the entire company, and this spectrum acquisition must be viewed in this context.
Incumbent carriers build dense networks of ground towers, using satellites as a supplementary option for remote areas. SpaceX is flipping this approach. They plan to use sky satellites as the baseline coverage and use ground towers merely as supplements for dense urban areas and inside buildings where satellite signals cannot reach.
Despite these grand plans, SpaceX faces real limitations. Cell towers beam signals from 1 km away, while satellites beam them from hundreds of kilometers above. Even switching to 800 MHz has physical limits. To match traditional carriers, SpaceX would need to build a dense network of ground towers—an enterprise requiring immense capital. SpaceX has proposed attaching mini base stations next to Starlink dishes, but industry experts largely view this as impractical.
Competition from Amazon adds further pressure. Amazon decided to acquire satellite communications company GlobalStar and declared it will launch phone satellite services starting in 2028. Furthermore, Apple selected Amazon—not SpaceX—to power satellite features on the iPhone (such as emergency SOS). SpaceX now finds itself caught between the three-carrier alliance and Amazon. Telecom is a business that demands endless capital investment in infrastructure, and AI is no different. SpaceX is effectively raising two cash-guzzling monsters at the same time.
One-Line Comment: The essence of Elon Musk's deal is securing Starlink's next growth engine. Because Starlink underpins the credit and financing of the entire group, it must keep growing. The stage for that growth is the U.S. mobile market, and the 800 MHz spectrum is the bare minimum key needed to enter it. However, while spectrum can be bought with a single contract, a telecommunications network requires years of massive investment and countless cell towers. It seems unlikely that Elon Musk will spend money building traditional towers; SpaceX's future hinges on whether it can find a viable alternative to traditional base stations.
