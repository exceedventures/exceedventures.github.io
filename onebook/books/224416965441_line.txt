The Anthropic Distillation Incident
On September 10, 2026, Anthropic released an intelligence report.
According to the report, Chinese AI labs—including Alibaba (Qwen), Moonshot AI (Kimi), and DeepSeek—carried out large-scale, unauthorized distillation attacks against Anthropic.
Distillation is a technique in which the responses generated by a high-performance AI model are used to train a smaller AI model. The problem was how they did it.
They allegedly created fake accounts using stolen credit cards and other payment information obtained from illegal underground markets, and then extracted massive amounts of responses from Anthropic's models.
I previously covered the details in an article titled "The AI War Between Anthropic and Alibaba." This time, however, Anthropic's report provides much more specific details.
Take Alibaba, for example. According to the report, more than 151 million distillation-related interactions were conducted between May and July 2026 using more than 3,500 fake accounts.
But then came the twist.
The Chinese AI companies got themselves into even bigger trouble.
Moonshot reportedly took legitimate requests submitted by users through Kimi, routed those requests to Claude for processing, and then used Claude's responses to train its own models.
From the user's perspective, the word "Kimi" was displayed on the screen, so they naturally thought they were communicating with a Chinese AI. In reality, however, their requests were being processed through Anthropic's servers in the United States.
One user who appeared to be affiliated with the Chinese People's Liberation Army reportedly asked the AI to analyze surveillance footage of military facilities, believing that they were using the Chinese AI service Kimi.
The user uploaded CCTV footage to the AI and asked it to determine whether the person under surveillance had exhibited any unusual behavior recently.
The user thought they were uploading the material to Kimi, a Chinese AI service. In reality, the data was uploaded to Anthropic.
The uploaded material reportedly consisted of footage from hundreds of CCTV cameras installed in Chengdu, including cameras monitoring facilities belonging to the People's Liberation Army.
From a counterintelligence perspective, the transfer of surveillance networks around sensitive military and security facilities to an external provider would constitute a serious security incident.
Another engineer using Kimi reportedly used the AI to help develop an internal system for a major state-owned enterprise.
During the process, the engineer entered internal source code from major Chinese companies, along with valid login credentials.
These were not expired accounts. They were actual usernames and passwords that could still be used to log in, meaning that the credentials were stored on servers operated by a U.S. company.
In theory, anyone with those credentials could potentially gain direct access to the companies' systems.
And it wasn't just Kimi. DeepSeek also got itself into trouble.
According to Anthropic, DeepSeek generated more than 12.1 million interactions with Anthropic's systems in just 14 days during July.
A Chinese technical employee who believed they were using DeepSeek reportedly uploaded documents containing extensive information about an AI program that was still under development.
But there was something even more serious.
Engineers working to improve systems used by Chinese public security authorities reportedly used DeepSeek to develop a system for monitoring citizens.
The system was reportedly designed to link citizens' national identification numbers with their movements, as detected through facial recognition from CCTV footage, as well as with police records.
In other words, if a police officer entered someone's national ID number, the system could potentially show where that person had been throughout the day based on information captured by CCTV facial-recognition systems.
Because the system was also connected to police records, it could potentially be used to determine whether someone's statements about their activities were inconsistent with those records.
These are just the cases Anthropic has publicly disclosed. Anthropic reportedly indicated that there were even more serious cases that it had not made public.
There are also reports that data belonging to Russian intelligence authorities was uploaded while using Chinese AI services. If that is true, both countries may now be scrambling to determine exactly what information was exposed.
One-line comment : Illegal distillation is one thing, but the idea that entering a citizen's national ID number could reveal their movements throughout the day through CCTV surveillance is something else entirely. China is certainly a unique case.
