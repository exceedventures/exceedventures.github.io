Title: The Story of Radish (feat. Dr. Woo Jang-choon, Seedless Watermelon, Autumn Radish, and Wintering Radish)
The origin of the radish is the Mediterranean coast.
It is estimated that radishes were introduced to Korea around the Three Kingdoms period via China.
Radishes that have adapted to Korea for over a thousand years are called 'Joseon heirloom radishes' (Joseon Jaeraemu).
The radishes sold at supermarkets generally fall into two categories.
One is the 'Joseon radish' (Joseon-mu), and the other is the 'Japanese radish' (Wae-mu).
As the name suggests, Wae-mu is Japanese radish.
The yellow pickled radish (Danmuji) inside Gimbap is made from Japanese radish, which spread in Korea along with the pickled radish culture during the Japanese colonial period.
During the Japanese colonial period, Korea was importing native radish seeds—known as 'Joseon radish'—from Japan.
The Japanese colonial government attempted to turn Korea into its food supply base by distributing improved Japanese rice varieties throughout the peninsula.
The adoption rate of improved Japanese rice varieties, which was only 5% in 1912, rose to 65% in 1920 and reached 86% by 1936.
Imperial Japan forced Korean agriculture to focus heavily on rice and barley, suppressing the growth of the local vegetable seed industry.
Consequently, seeds for Napa cabbage and radishes—the essential ingredients for making kimchi—had to be imported from Japan.
Radishes and Napa cabbages are not crops where you can simply save a portion of the harvest to replant next season, unlike rice.
Radishes and cabbages are grown for their roots or leaves; to harvest seeds, they must be cultivated separately until they flower and bear fruit.
This meant radish and cabbage seeds had to be purchased every year, and at the time, the sole supplier was Japan.
Following liberation in 1945, vegetable seed imports from Japan suddenly stopped, disrupting the seed supply and quickly escalating into a crisis directly tied to public survival.
Meanwhile, Dr. Woo Jang-choon had been researching the breeding of vegetable seeds like radishes and cabbages at the Takii Seed Company research farm in Japan since 1937.
Born to a Korean father and a Japanese mother, Dr. Woo returned to Korea alone on March 8, 1950, leaving his family, including his Japanese wife, behind in Japan.
The committee advocating for his return sent 1 million yen to support his family's living expenses, but Dr. Woo used that money to buy laboratory equipment and agricultural seeds to bring with him to Korea instead.
In 1954, he began producing radish and cabbage seeds in Jindo, Jeollanam-do, and succeeded in mass-producing them by 1957.
When people hear the name Dr. Woo Jang-choon, they often associate him with the seedless watermelon.
However, the seedless watermelon was actually first developed in 1943 by Dr. Hitoshi Kihara of Kyoto University in Japan.
Dr. Woo merely produced seedless watermelons in Korea to demonstrate and promote the importance of seed development, leading to the misconception that he was the inventor.
If you learned in school textbooks that Dr. Woo Jang-choon invented the seedless watermelon, it’s a sign of your age!
That is because the textbooks were revised in the 1980s.
Upon his return, President Syngman Rhee offered Dr. Woo the position of Minister of Agriculture and Forestry, but he declined and dedicated all his energy solely to achieving seed independence.
Initially, Jeju Island was proposed as the location for Dr. Woo’s seed breeding test site.
However, an on-site evaluation revealed that Jeju's warm climate and early rainy season made it difficult to produce high-quality seeds, so the test site was moved to Jindo.
Instead, he determined that Jeju was ideal for growing mandarins and recommended citrus farming, while also bringing rapeseed (Yuchae) seeds from Japan to Jeju.
This is why Jeju Island is now famous for its mandarins and canola (Yuchae) flowers.
After returning to Korea, Dr. Woo was never able to visit his family in Japan until the day he died.
In June 1953, upon receiving news that his mother was critically ill, he attempted to go to Japan, but the government refused to issue him a passport out of fear that he might not return.
His mother passed away on August 18 of that year, and his disciples set up a memorial altar in the research institute's auditorium for him to pay his respects.
Dr. Woo used the condolence money he received to dig a well at the research institute, naming it Jayucheon ("Mother's Merciful Milk").
He was unable to return to Japan to see his family until his death in 1959; his Japanese wife arrived in Korea only after hearing he was critically ill to stay by his bedside in his final moments.
The optimal temperature for Joseon radishes to grow well is around 15–20°C (59–68°F).
As autumn sets in, radishes start to develop a sweet flavor—the secret behind this sweetness lies in the radish’s struggle to avoid freezing.
As the temperature drops, the radish begins storing sugar inside its cells to prevent them from freezing and bursting.
Based on the same principle that sugar water freezes at a lower temperature than plain water, the radish produces its own natural antifreeze.
Autumn also brings a noticeable change in texture.
As temperatures fall, the inner tissue of the radish becomes much denser and firmer.
Although radishes are sold at the market year-round, they are not all the same type.
The varieties shift throughout the year: Spring Radish (May–July), Alpine Radish from Gangwon-do (July–October), Autumn Radish (October–November), and Wintering Radish (December–March).
Even among autumn radishes, the taste varies over time.
The rich sweetness only fully develops by mid-November.
From December onward, Jeju Wintering Radishes take over, and through March of the following year, both late autumn radishes and Jeju wintering radishes deliver a sweetness that rivals fresh fruit.
Personally, I start eating raw radish around mid-November.
For about a month starting mid-November, I enjoy autumn radish, and as the end of the year approaches, I switch to Jeju wintering radish.
The upper green portion of the radish—the part that grows above ground—is the sweetest and most delicious part.
If you harvest radish greens (mucheong) between October and December and hang them in a well-ventilated area, they will freeze and thaw repeatedly throughout the winter, naturally drying into Siraegi (dried radish greens).
Siraegi made from radish greens is practically a natural supplement, rich in dietary fiber, calcium, potassium, and folate.
When grandmothers in the old days hung remaining radish greens under the eaves after making kimchi, it wasn't just to avoid waste.
It was wisdom passed down to replenish essential nutrients like calcium, iron, and dietary fiber during harsh winters when fresh vegetables were scarce.
Siraegi pairs exceptionally well with mackerel: mackerel provides the protein and unsaturated fatty acids that Siraegi lacks, while Siraegi neutralizes the fishy odor of the mackerel.
A restaurant named Namgang in Seosomun-dong used to make a fantastic braised mackerel with Siraegi (Siraegi-godeungeo-jorim), but it seems to have closed down, which is a real shame.
The Siraegi braised mackerel at Hyoje-dong Jorim-jip in Jongno is also quite good, though they are closed on Sundays.
One-line Comment: Dr. Woo Jang-choon left behind so many achievements that it is impossible to talk about the Korean seed industry without mentioning him. I wonder if he ever regretted returning to Korea until the very end. If it's hard to get braised mackerel with Siraegi, maybe I should at least go for Ugeoji Gamjatang (pork bone soup with dried cabbage leaves) instead of radish greens.
