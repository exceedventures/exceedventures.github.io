The Secret Behind Nike’s Decline
Nike has been removed from the S&P 100, so I thought it would be worth taking a look at what happened.
On September 21, 2026, Nike was removed from the S&P 100 after 18 years. Its spot, along with those of other companies removed from the index, was taken by companies such as Dell and SanDisk.
The reason Nike was dropped from the S&P 100 was that its market capitalization fell below the required threshold after years of declining stock prices.
Nike’s stock price, which reached $179 in 2021, has fallen by about 80% to around $35.
Even just looking at this year, Nike’s stock is down about 40%, putting it near the bottom of the Dow Jones.
There are many reasons behind Nike’s decline, but I want to focus on three.
1. The decision to abandon wholesale channels
In 2020, during the COVID-19 pandemic, Nike was one of the biggest beneficiaries of the changing consumer environment.
As physical stores closed, consumers flocked to Nike.com. Because Nike was able to sell directly to consumers online, it generated record-level profits.
At the time, CEO John Donahoe saw the rapid growth of Nike.com and made a decisive bet.
Nike set a goal of generating more than 60% of its sales through its online channel, Nike.com, and its own retail stores. In the process, it began cutting ties with many of its long-standing wholesale partners.
Nike’s critical mistake was believing that the pandemic had permanently changed consumer behavior and that physical stores were essentially dead.
The problem was that consumers still wanted to go to stores, try on shoes, and buy them in person.
Once the pandemic ended, Nike’s online sales began to decline. And because Nike had cut ties with many wholesale partners, other brands began taking up the shelf space that Nike had once occupied.
While Nike was abandoning wholesale channels and restructuring itself around online sales, brands such as On Running, Hoka, and New Balance began taking market share.
2. Falling behind in product development
Another problem was that Nike became so focused on expanding online sales that it failed to devote enough attention to product development.
A running shoe basically has a three-layer structure.
The outsole is the rubber layer that comes into contact with the ground and is responsible for traction and durability.
The upper is the part that wraps around the top of the foot.
Between the outsole and the foot is the midsole, which absorbs impact when your foot lands and helps propel you forward.
Most of the technological innovation in running shoes happens in the midsole, and each brand gives its own technology a distinctive name.
Nike uses names such as ZoomX and Air Zoom. Adidas has Boost and Lightstrike, while ASICS uses FF BLAST.
Competitors tend to overhaul their midsoles every year. Nike, however, has been replacing its midsole technology on roughly a two-year cycle, while making mostly cosmetic changes to the colors and uppers in between.
For consumers, the part of a shoe that makes it feel genuinely new is often the change in the midsole technology.
Nike’s product-development cycle simply became too slow.
Eventually, Nike found itself relying heavily on changing the colors and designs of previously popular products rather than introducing truly new products.
As a result, consumers gradually began losing interest.
3. When Nike started selling beliefs instead of products
At some point, Nike also began selling beliefs rather than simply selling products.
When a brand starts selling a social or political message, sales can often increase in the short term. But it can also damage the brand’s image and create a larger group of people who oppose the brand.
In 2016, the United States was deeply divided and angry as incidents involving unarmed Black people being killed by police spread through smartphone footage, body-camera videos, and social media.
In American football, the quarterback is the face and leader of the team, roughly comparable to a star pitcher in baseball.
Colin Kaepernick, a superstar quarterback who had led his team to the Super Bowl, was the starting quarterback for the San Francisco 49ers.
Before sporting events, players and spectators traditionally stand and place their hands over their hearts while the national anthem is played.
When the U.S. national anthem began playing, Kaepernick remained seated, saying that he would not show respect to a flag representing a country that discriminated against people based on race.
As the negative perception of remaining seated during the national anthem grew, he changed his protest to kneeling on one knee.
This may be difficult for Koreans to fully relate to, but in the United States, the American flag and the national anthem are powerful symbols associated with the sacrifices of soldiers, patriotism, and America itself.
Regardless of Kaepernick’s intentions, kneeling instead of standing was interpreted by some Americans as an insult to the flag, the military, and the country.
A fierce debate began between those who saw the protest as a legitimate demonstration against racism and police violence and those who saw it as disrespectful to the country and its military.
Nike entered the controversy.
The company made Kaepernick the face of an advertising campaign and used the following slogan:
“Believe in something. Even if it means sacrificing everything.”
It was essentially Nike’s public declaration that positioned the brand at the forefront of political correctness.
Immediately after the Kaepernick campaign, Nike’s online sales increased 31% compared with the same period a year earlier, and the campaign was widely regarded as a short-term success.
From that point on, Nike began actively incorporating messages related to race, gender, and social diversity into its brand marketing.
The industry refers to this as inclusive marketing.
Inclusive marketing is often viewed as the commercialized version of political correctness.
Political correctness is based on the idea that the way a particular group is named and represented can influence that group’s actual social status.
For example, replacing “policeman” with “police officer,” or replacing terms such as “Negro” and “colored” with “Black” or “African American.”
Brands and sports teams have also changed their names for similar reasons. For example, Major League Baseball’s Cleveland Indians changed their name to the Guardians.
Nike began inclusive marketing campaigns featuring people of different races and body types rather than focusing primarily on thin white models.
Plus-size marketing also became part of Nike’s strategy.
This went beyond simply marketing to customers who needed larger sizes. Nike placed plus-size mannequins in its stores and launched major advertising campaigns around body diversity.
In the short term, plus-size marketing increased sales of larger-size clothing, but it also became one of the reasons some groups of customers developed a more negative perception of the Nike brand.
In October 2024, CEO John Donahoe stepped down, taking responsibility for the company’s weak sales performance, and Elliott Hill became the new CEO.
The new CEO began rebuilding Nike’s wholesale distribution network and shifted the company’s marketing focus away from inclusive messaging and back toward the products themselves.
Some people remain unimpressed by Nike’s changes, while others maintain a negative view, saying that the company is simply putting on a show because its sales have fallen.
One-line comment
There was nothing inherently wrong with Nike’s marketing strategy itself. Population growth in the United States has been driven largely by non-white demographic groups, and the high obesity rate means that plus-size consumers represent a significant market.
The problem was that Nike moved beyond selling products and increasingly connected its brand to social and political issues.
With a new CEO, Nike is returning to its traditional product-focused approach, but it currently seems to be in a position where neither side is fully satisfied.
Corporate marketing of this kind can generate attention in the short term and strengthen loyalty among certain groups. However, in a society where polarization between opposing sides is becoming increasingly intense, it can also create a risk of increasing resistance to the brand.
From an investment perspective, Nike does have one potential advantage.
Because the stock price has fallen so sharply, its dividend yield has naturally risen to around 4.4–4.6%.
Nike has now become the highest-yielding dividend stock in the Dow Jones.
