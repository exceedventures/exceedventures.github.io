A New Source of Energy: Fusion Power Is Moving Faster
In 1905, at the age of 26, Albert Einstein published his theory of special relativity.
One of the most famous equations to emerge from this work was the mass–energy equivalence principle, E = mc².
This principle became the theoretical foundation of nuclear fusion.
When deuterium and tritium collide, they form a helium nucleus.
The mass of the resulting helium nucleus is slightly smaller than the combined mass of the deuterium and tritium.
In other words, some mass has been lost.
According to the mass–energy equivalence principle, that missing mass is converted into energy, producing energetic neutrons and other forms of kinetic energy.
In a fusion reactor, the neutrons produced by fusion strike the reactor walls and generate heat. That heat boils water to produce steam, which then drives turbines and generates electricity.
The Sun is essentially a natural fusion power plant.
Fusion power is an attempt to reproduce the energy-generation process that powers the Sun here on Earth and use it to generate electricity.
It sounds simple, but there is a major challenge: to achieve fusion, atomic nuclei have to be forced together.
Imagine trying to push two magnets together with the same poles facing each other. They naturally repel one another.
If you apply a force greater than the repulsive force of the magnets, you can temporarily push them together.
Fusion works on a similar principle. If enough energy is supplied to overcome the electrostatic repulsion between atomic nuclei, a fusion reaction can occur.
Inside the Sun, the pressure at the core is so enormous that fusion can occur at temperatures of around 10 million degrees Celsius.
There is nowhere on Earth with pressure comparable to the Sun's core, so we need temperatures of more than 100 million degrees Celsius to achieve fusion.
When deuterium and tritium are heated to more than 100 million degrees, they become a state of matter in which atomic nuclei and electrons are separated. This is called plasma.
Once the plasma reaches the right conditions, atomic nuclei begin to fuse and neutrons are produced.
If the plasma satisfies certain conditions, the fusion reaction can become self-sustaining without continuously supplying external heat—essentially creating an artificial Sun.
There are two major approaches to fusion fuel being pursued today: one uses deuterium and tritium, while the other uses deuterium and helium-3.
Deuterium is relatively easy to obtain. About 0.03 grams can be extracted from one liter of seawater.
The real challenge is obtaining tritium and helium-3.
Tritium barely exists naturally. It is produced as a byproduct of nuclear fission in reactors that use heavy water.
The CANDU reactors in Canada and South Korea's Wolsong Nuclear Power Plant use heavy water and produce tritium.
Tritium is used in a wide range of applications, including nuclear weapon systems, emergency-exit signs, self-luminous devices, and life-science research.
Tritium is extremely expensive, selling for roughly $30,000 per gram at wholesale and up to $120,000 per gram at retail—around 170 million Korean won.
It is not only expensive but also extremely scarce. As of 2025, only about 25 kilograms of tritium was estimated to be stored worldwide.
Of that 25 kilograms, South Korea holds approximately 5.7 kilograms, and the Wolsong nuclear plant produces around 700 grams per year.
CANDU reactors exist not only in South Korea and Canada but also in countries such as India, China, and Romania.
However, South Korea and Canada have something the others do not: TRF, or Tritium Removal Facilities.
TRFs are facilities that separate and purify tritium produced during the operation of nuclear reactors.
That is one of the reasons why South Korea and Canada are the primary countries capable of supplying tritium.
Global tritium inventories are expected to become increasingly scarce, with total stocks projected to peak at around 28 kilograms in 2030.
There are two main reasons: the Wolsong reactors in South Korea are beginning to shut down, while demand for tritium is expected to increase rapidly.
The EU's planned DEMO (DEMOnstration Fusion Power Plant) is expected to require approximately 5 kilograms of tritium just to begin operation.
Wolsong Nuclear Power Plant has four CANDU reactors, Units 1 through 4.
Unit 1 has already undergone approximately 1 trillion won worth of refurbishment for a potential life extension, but its permanent shutdown and decommissioning have nevertheless been decided. The process of selecting a decommissioning contractor is underway.
Decommissioning is expected to begin in the first half of 2027.
The design lives of Wolsong Units 2, 3, and 4 expire in November 2026, December 2027, and February 2029, respectively.
Applications for life extensions of Units 2 through 4 were submitted in June 2024 and are still under review.
Unit 2, whose design life expires this November, is the most urgent case.
Even if its life extension is approved, the reactor reportedly needs to replace its Canadian-made pressure tubes in order to continue operating.
The problem is that the order for those pressure tubes can only be placed after the life-extension approval.
That makes sense from a government procurement perspective. It would be difficult for officials to spend a large amount of public money before receiving regulatory approval for continued operation.
The Canadian supplier reportedly says that even if an order were placed now, delivery would not be possible until 2029, according to Korea Hydro & Nuclear Power (KHNP).
This means that even if the life extension is approved, the Wolsong reactors could potentially remain offline for several years.
As reactors that produce tritium shut down and supplies become increasingly scarce, the United States and China are also pursuing another approach: fusion using helium-3.
There is a major difference between tritium–deuterium fusion and helium-3–deuterium fusion.
Tritium fusion can occur at around 100 million degrees Celsius, whereas helium-3 requires temperatures of more than 600 million degrees Celsius.
Helium-3 has one more proton than tritium, creating a much stronger electrostatic repulsion between nuclei.
Just as stronger magnets are harder to push together when their like poles face each other, helium-3 fusion is considerably more difficult than tritium fusion.
Current fusion research is therefore moving in two broad directions.
The first is to continue using tritium while developing technology to recycle and breed more tritium because supplies are limited.
If the plasma is surrounded by lithium, the high-energy neutrons produced by fusion can collide with the lithium and generate new tritium.
Theoretically, this process could produce around 15% more tritium than the reactor consumes.
Once processing losses are taken into account, however, that would provide only a very narrow margin for keeping the reactor running continuously.
The concept is theoretically feasible, but it has not yet been demonstrated at the required engineering scale, so there is still a long way to go.
The other approach is to use helium-3.
On May 26, 2026, NASA announced plans for a Moon Base.
NASA aims to return humans to the lunar surface in 2028.
Unlike the Apollo missions of the 1960s, which involved relatively short visits, the goal is to build a permanent lunar base capable of supporting long-term habitation.
The lunar base the United States is envisioning is larger than many people might expect.
It would cover approximately 300 square miles, or 777 square kilometers.
For comparison, Seoul has an area of roughly 605 square kilometers, meaning the planned area would be about 1.3 times the size of Seoul.
Of course, this does not mean the entire area will be covered with buildings. It refers to the broader zone containing landing sites, solar power facilities, habitation modules, and other infrastructure.
There is no conventional legal ownership of lunar territory, but establishing a base could provide a form of practical priority or strategic presence. That appears to be one reason the United States wants to secure as large an area as possible.
In 2028, the first crewed lunar landing since Apollo is expected to take place, followed by more serious efforts toward short-term habitation beginning in 2029.
NASA has also presented a vision for a “lunar economy.”
The NASA administrator has said that the goal is to create a lunar economy and that continuous space exploration cannot rely solely on taxpayer funding.
In other words, the goal is to make money on the Moon.
One potential revenue model is a space gas station.
The idea is to extract ice from the Moon, separate it into hydrogen and oxygen, turn it into rocket fuel, and sell that fuel to private spacecraft.
The second potential business is mining helium-3 from the lunar surface.
The solar wind carries helium-3 through space, but Earth's magnetic field and atmosphere prevent most of it from reaching the surface.
The Moon has neither a global magnetic field nor an atmosphere, so helium-3 from the solar wind has accumulated in the lunar soil over billions of years.
The basic idea is to excavate lunar regolith, heat it, and extract the helium-3 contained within it.
The company pursuing this commercially is Interlune.
Interlune, together with industrial equipment manufacturer Vermeer, has unveiled a prototype excavator designed to process 100 tons of lunar regolith per hour.
The company has announced a roadmap targeting a demonstration on the Moon in 2027 and its first commercial mining operation in 2029.
However, the helium-3 Interlune plans to bring back to Earth is not primarily intended for fusion.
Interlune has signed a supply agreement with Finnish cryogenic cooling equipment company Bluefors to provide approximately 1.3 kilograms of lunar helium-3 per year from 2028 through 2037.
The contract is reportedly worth around $300 million and is intended for use in quantum computing, because helium-3 is essential for cooling quantum computers to temperatures close to absolute zero.
As Google, IBM, Intel, Amazon, and Microsoft scale up their quantum-computing programs, demand for helium-3 is expected to surge.
The U.S. Department of Energy has also signed a contract with Interlune for fusion research, but the initial commitment is only to deliver 0.4 grams at market price in 2029.
If Interlune succeeds in bringing helium-3 back to Earth, it expects the material could eventually command a price of around $20 million per kilogram.
The helium-3 is expected to be transported from the Moon to Earth using SpaceX's Starship.
Apollo landed a lunar module carrying two astronauts and several hundred kilograms of equipment.
Starship, by contrast, is designed to land nearly 100 tons of people and cargo on the Moon.
Even if helium-3 can be secured, however, another enormous challenge remains: heating it to 600 million degrees Celsius and maintaining a stable plasma at that temperature.
One of the companies currently making particularly rapid progress in fusion is Commonwealth Fusion Systems (CFS).
CFS is a company that spun out of MIT. It is building a fusion demonstration machine called SPARC in Devens, Massachusetts, and the project is reportedly about 75% complete.
On July 1, 2025, Google signed an agreement to purchase 200 megawatts of fusion energy from CFS.
CFS is also developing a 400-megawatt commercial fusion power plant called ARC in Virginia, meaning Google has effectively secured half of the plant's planned output.
CFS, which was founded out of MIT, proposed in 2021 that it was theoretically possible to build a fusion system capable of achieving net energy gain, producing more energy than the amount supplied to the system.
Investors including Bill Gates and Amazon founder Jeff Bezos have invested around $2 billion in CFS to help turn that theory into a commercial fusion power plant.
Other major CFS investors include Nvidia, Stanley Druckenmiller, and former Google CEO Eric Schmidt.
CFS is targeting operation of its SPARC demonstration machine by the end of 2027, while simultaneously developing the 400-megawatt ARC plant in Virginia, which would be capable of supplying electricity to approximately 280,000 homes.
Another company is Helion Energy.
OpenAI's Sam Altman serves as its chairman and largest shareholder and has invested $375 million of his personal wealth in the company.
Helion is also accelerating commercialization with backing and participation from people and organizations including Palantir's Peter Thiel, NASA, the U.S. Department of Energy, and the U.S. Department of Defense.
CFS uses tritium, while Helion is pursuing a helium-3-based approach, meaning Helion is taking on a technically more difficult challenge.
The grand old name in private fusion research is TAE Technologies.
Founded in California in 1998, TAE has received approximately $1.3 billion in investment from companies and investors including Google, Chevron Technology Ventures, and others.
Fusion energy is also receiving strong support under Trump's second administration.
On June 9, 2026, the U.S. Department of Energy announced its final roadmap for the commercialization of fusion energy.
A newly established Office of Fusion will oversee implementation of the roadmap.
The goal is to build fusion power plants through public-private cooperation during the 2030s.
TAE is collaborating with the U.S. Department of Energy, Google, UCLA, and others, and holds more than 1,500 fusion-related patents.
There is also a reason Trump might have a particular interest in fusion.
On December 18, 2025, TAE and Trump Media & Technology Group (TMTG) announced plans to merge.
The transaction is structured as a stock-for-stock merger, with shareholders of the two companies ultimately holding shares representing roughly half of the combined company.
TMTG would also provide $300 million in cash to TAE, creating a structure in which cash from Trump's media company would be used to help commercialize TAE's fusion technology.
According to an SEC filing made in August, the companies are targeting regulatory approval of the merger during the fourth quarter of 2026 and aim to secure a site and begin construction of a power plant within 2026.
On August 5, 2026, TMTG also disclosed that it had signed an agreement with Black Moon Energy to secure helium-3 for fusion applications, suggesting that the company is also exploring helium-3-based fusion.
TMTG is the media company founded by Trump in 2021 and currently operates Truth Social, which serves as Trump's primary social-media platform.
In addition to Truth Social, the company has also expanded into areas such as cryptocurrency-related investments.
After becoming president, Trump transferred his assets into the Donald J. Trump Revocable Trust.
Trump previously owned approximately 52% of TMTG, and that stake was also transferred to the trust.
Trump himself is the beneficiary of the trust, while his son Donald Trump Jr. serves as trustee.
Voting rights and investment authority over the shares held by the trust are exercised by Trump Jr., while the economic benefits ultimately go to Trump.
In other words, although the assets are held through a trust structure, they retain much of the character of Trump's personal wealth.
This creates an unusual situation in which a company whose largest shareholder is the sitting president is merging with a fusion company operating in an industry that the U.S. government is actively promoting.
The New York Times and others have raised questions about potential conflicts of interest.
Helium-3 is abundant on the Moon, but it is also produced on Earth.
The helium-3 found on Earth is generated as a byproduct of tritium decay.
Since tritium itself is extremely scarce, helium-3 is naturally scarce as well.
The United States produces less than one kilogram of helium-3 per year, which is one reason large-scale future applications may eventually require helium-3 imported from the Moon.
The remaining major challenge for fusion is therefore maintaining stable plasma for a sufficiently long period of time.
China is pursuing fusion power as a national strategic project and has set a goal of becoming the world's leading fusion nation by 2028.
In January 2026, China announced plans to build a fusion science and technology innovation demonstration zone in Hefei's Changfeng County, integrating a research park, industrial cluster, and residential facilities.
China aims to begin transmitting commercially generated fusion electricity to the actual power grid from 2035.
Construction of BEST, its research and demonstration fusion reactor, began in 2023.
BEST is targeted for completion by the end of 2027. Once demonstration testing is completed, China plans to build the much larger CFETR (China Fusion Engineering Test Reactor).
China is accelerating its research by deploying more than ten times as many PhD-level researchers as the United States reportedly assigns to fusion research, while operating research teams around the clock in three shifts.
At the EAST superconducting tokamak in Hefei, China has successfully maintained plasma for 1,066 seconds—about 17 minutes—setting a world record.
The already completed EAST tokamak is being used for physical validation and experiments.
The current schedule is to complete BEST in 2027, demonstrate sustained plasma operation, and then move on to construction of CFETR.
One-line comment
There is an old joke about fusion energy: whenever someone asks when fusion will become commercially viable, the answer is always “30 years from now,” regardless of when the question is asked.
Fusion, which has always been described as a technology that is "30 years away," is now being accompanied by concrete roadmaps targeting commercialization within the next decade.
CFS is pursuing a relatively stable commercialization strategy—essentially making existing technology smaller, stronger, and more practical—while Helion is taking a radically different approach and attempting to achieve fusion in an entirely new way.
Both companies are competing for the title of the world's first commercial fusion power plant, with their current targets falling somewhere between 2027 and 2028.
TAE has also joined the race, turning it into a three-way competition.
And given that a company personally associated with Trump is merging with a fusion company, it certainly seems likely that government support for the industry will remain strong.
