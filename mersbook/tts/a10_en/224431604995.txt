The Most Dangerous Element in the World
A black mineral was first discovered during the mining of a silver mine in the Ore Mountains of Germany.
Whenever this black mineral appeared, the vein of silver in the mine would suddenly disappear. The miners named the black mineral pitchblende—from Pech, meaning “bad luck,” and Blende, meaning “mineral” or “ore”—and simply threw it away as waste whenever they found it.
In the silver mines where pitchblende was found, hundreds of miners died from a disease that caused them to cough up blood and severely damaged their lungs.
So many miners in the region continued to die that there is even a record of one miner's widow marrying as many as seven times because her husbands repeatedly died.
At the time, the medical community believed that the miners' mass deaths were caused by toxic gases inside the mines.
In 1898, Marie and Pierre Curie succeeded in discovering the presence of a substance in pitchblende that emitted intense radiation.
They named this highly radioactive substance polonium, after Poland, Marie Curie's homeland.
It took quite some time before people understood the dangers of the radiation emitted by pitchblende.
Marie Curie eventually died of aplastic anemia caused by radiation exposure.
Even today, some of Marie Curie's belongings, including her research notebooks and even her cookbooks, are still radioactive, so the National Library of France stores them in lead-lined boxes.
Anyone wishing to examine her notebooks or other belongings must still wear protective equipment and sign a liability waiver.
Pitchblende was also used in the production of atomic bombs.
The Manhattan Project, through which the United States developed the atomic bomb, used 34,200 tons of pitchblende mined in the Congo as a source of raw material.
Yasser Arafat, chairman of the Palestine Liberation Organization, became ill during dinner on October 12, 2004. After vomiting, he lost consciousness and fell into a coma.
As his condition continued to deteriorate, the French government transported him by air to the Percy Military Hospital near Paris on October 29, but he eventually died.
An autopsy was not performed because his wife opposed it, so the exact cause of his death remained unknown.
In 2012, a laboratory in Lausanne, Switzerland, detected abnormally high levels of polonium-210 in personal belongings belonging to Arafat, including his toothbrush and clothing.
Arafat, the chairman of the PLO, was a moderate and relatively peace-oriented leader.
His moderate approach was unpopular with both Israel and Palestinian hardliners such as Hamas.
Israel believed it would be easier to deal with the PLO if the organization took a harder line.
From Hamas's perspective, Arafat was also problematic because he advocated achieving Palestinian independence through peace and negotiations.
In September 2003, the Israeli cabinet decided to remove Arafat, and then-Deputy Prime Minister Ehud Olmert publicly stated that killing him was one possible option.
When Arafat subsequently died under mysterious circumstances, rumors circulated that Israel's Mossad had provided Hamas with polonium-210 so that Arafat could be assassinated.
Polonium has 42 known isotopes.
Polonium is the name of the element as a whole, while polonium-210 is one of its 42 isotopes and is particularly deadly to humans.
Polonium-210 has a half-life of about 138 days, so it does not have a major long-term impact on the environment.
Because it emits alpha particles, which have very low penetrating power and travel only a very short distance, simply touching polonium-210 does not normally cause a fatal level of exposure.
The real danger comes when it enters the human body.
Once inside the body, it damages organs and tissues, including the blood and bone marrow, until it is eventually excreted through urine or feces.
The lethal dose is said to be around one microgram, or one-millionth of a gram. In theory, one gram could be enough to kill one million people.
Alexander Litvinenko, a Russian intelligence officer who defected to Britain in 2000, was poisoned while exposing secrets about Putin.
After drinking green tea at Pine Bar in London's Millennium Hotel, he developed unexplained symptoms of poisoning and died three weeks after being hospitalized.
London police detected polonium-210 in Litvinenko's urine and found the same substance in a teapot left at the hotel.
Although he drank green tea, the incident came to be known as a British tea-poisoning case.
If a small amount of colorless and tasteless polonium-210 is added to hot tea and someone is made to drink it, it effectively places an extremely powerful radioactive bomb inside the person's body.
Touching it is not necessarily a major problem because the skin acts as a protective barrier. But once it enters the body, it can have devastating effects, making it a potentially effective substance for assassination.
Polonium-210 is produced primarily in nuclear reactors.
In principle, polonium-210 can be produced wherever there is a reactor capable of producing neutrons. The real question, however, is whether it can be produced secretly.
Commercial production of polonium-210 has historically been handled by the Avangard plant in Sarov, Russia, which has supplied the substance internationally.
Israel has a military reactor that is not subject to IAEA inspections, meaning that it is a country capable of producing polonium-210 without external monitoring.
Polonium-210 can also occur naturally.
Radioactive radium produced inside the Earth decays and produces polonium-210, which can become incorporated into phosphate rock.
Fertilizer can be made from phosphate rock containing polonium-210.
And there is a plant whose leaves can readily accumulate substances from such fertilizer.
That plant is tobacco.
The tiny, sticky hairs on tobacco leaves concentrate polonium-210. During smoking, the tobacco is heated, and the polonium is not easily eliminated from the body, allowing it to remain in the lungs.
Cigarettes produced in countries such as South Korea, the United States, China, and Germany are said to contain approximately 280–580 millibecquerels of polonium-210 per pack.
Almost no polonium is detected in tobacco leaves grown in India.
This appears to be because India uses very little synthetic fertilizer in tobacco cultivation.
Smoking, as well as exposure to secondhand smoke, causes polonium to accumulate in the bronchial tubes.
Polonium-210 is also detected in the bronchial tubes of nonsmokers, which is believed to be a result of secondhand smoke exposure.
Tobacco companies have known since the 1960s that polonium-210 is present in tobacco.
They have tried to address the issue by washing tobacco leaves, using filters, and developing genetically modified tobacco, but the problem has still not been fully resolved.
It has been confirmed that acid washing can substantially reduce the amount of polonium, but the method has not been adopted because it can affect nicotine levels and flavor, as well as raise concerns about public controversy.
The current argument made by tobacco companies is essentially that radioactive substances naturally present in the environment cannot be avoided.
It is estimated that around 11,700 people worldwide develop lung cancer each year as a result of polonium-210 in tobacco.
