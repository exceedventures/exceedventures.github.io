Is the Red Sea Becoming More Dangerous? (feat. Cooperation Between Extremist Groups from the Sunni and Shia Camps)

The Houthi rebels, who currently control Yemen's capital, Sana'a, as well as other major areas, are part of the Shia branch of Islam.
The territory controlled by the Houthis accounts for only about one-third of Yemen's total land area. However, because they control many of the country's densely populated areas, they effectively control more than 60% of Yemen's population.
Yemen is not home to only the Shia Houthi rebels. There is also AQAP (Al-Qaeda in the Arabian Peninsula), the Yemeni branch of the Sunni extremist organization Al-Qaeda.
In AQAP's worldview, Shia Muslims are not simply members of another Islamic sect. They are considered apostates.
AQAP's view of apostates is even more extreme than the way some Christian groups might regard heretical religious groups.
The concept known as takfir, the declaration that someone is an apostate, can go as far as declaring that the person is legitimately killable, thereby being used to justify murder.
AQAP was officially established by Nasir al-Wuhayshi, a former personal secretary to Osama bin Laden, who brought together remnants of Al-Qaeda's forces in Yemen.
Through a series of terrorist activities, AQAP came to be regarded by the U.S. intelligence community as one of the most dangerous branches of Al-Qaeda.
In 2009, AQAP attempted to detonate a bomb hidden in a passenger's underwear on a flight bound for Detroit.
In 2010, it attempted to place disguised bombs on cargo planes bound for the United States.
AQAP also claimed responsibility for the 2015 Charlie Hebdo attack in Paris, in which 12 people were killed.
Repeated terrorist attacks led AQAP to be regarded as one of the world's most dangerous terrorist organizations.
As the Houthis expanded their territory into south-central Yemen, areas that had traditionally been within AQAP's sphere of influence, AQAP declared a sectarian war and began resisting the Houthi expansion.
AQAP continued its resistance against the Houthis using one of its signature tactics: suicide bombings.
In 2021, Al-Bayda, one of AQAP's key strongholds, fell to the Houthis.
Then, in July 2022, the two sides reached an agreement.
They agreed not to attack each other and to regard Yemen's government forces—and Saudi Arabia, the United States, and Israel, which support the Yemeni government—as common enemies.
The Houthis began cooperating with AQAP, including releasing approximately 70 AQAP prisoners.
Now look at the lower end of the Red Sea, around the Gulf of Aden, where one side is bordered by Somalia in Africa and the other by Yemen.
Somalia fell into anarchy after dictator Siad Barre was overthrown in 1991, causing the central government to collapse.
A coalition of warlords took control of the capital, but in 2006, Islamist groups drove the warlords out and seized Mogadishu.
Among these Islamist organizations was an especially radical and hard-line militant group called Al-Shabaab, which means "the youth" in Arabic.
In 2012, Al-Shabaab pledged allegiance to Al-Qaeda and formally became one of its affiliates.
So, within the Al-Qaeda network, Yemen has AQAP while Somalia has Al-Shabaab.
After joining Al-Qaeda, Al-Shabaab carried out a series of terrorist attacks to establish its presence within the organization.
In 2013, four gunmen held a siege at the Westgate shopping mall in Nairobi for four days, killing 67 people.
In 2015, Al-Shabaab fighters infiltrated Garissa University in Kenya and killed 148 Christian students.
In 2016, Al-Shabaab attempted to detonate a laptop bomb aboard a Daallo Airlines flight. However, the explosion was too weak, and only the bomber was killed.
On October 14, 2017, a truck bomb in Mogadishu killed 587 people, making it the deadliest terrorist attack in Somali history.
True to its name—"the youth"—Al-Shabaab remains highly active in terrorist operations.
The Somali government has been strengthening its forces and consolidating control over the capital, but Al-Shabaab still retains considerable power and remains a formidable opponent.
Al-Shabaab's real weapon is not bombs.
It is money.
The group collects taxes from farmers, businesses, livestock owners, roads, ports, and other economic activities, effectively operating a governing structure similar to that of a government.
Al-Shabaab even collects taxes in areas it does not directly control, including parts of the capital Mogadishu, which are under government control.
It has effectively become a "shadow state."
Some estimates suggest that roughly one-third of Al-Shabaab's revenue comes from Mogadishu alone.
The Somali federal government's annual revenue is estimated at around $250 million, while Al-Shabaab is believed to collect roughly $200 million.
This is why the Combating Terrorism Center (CTC) at West Point has described Al-Shabaab as Al-Qaeda's most financially successful affiliate.
If Al-Shabaab's strength is its financial resources, its weakness is its relatively poor access to weapons.
In this respect, it is almost the exact opposite of the Houthis.
The Houthis have relatively limited financial resources but are heavily armed thanks to weapons and other support from Iran and other sources.
Because the Houthis can obtain weapons relatively easily through Iranian support and other channels, weapons originating from Yemen are not particularly expensive.
Somalia, on the other hand, has limited access to weapons, making them roughly five times more expensive than in Yemen.
This is where AQAP, the Al-Qaeda affiliate in Yemen, comes in.
AQAP has reportedly acted as a broker, facilitating arms transactions between the Houthis and Al-Shabaab.
The basic deal is simple:
The Houthis sell weapons to Al-Shabaab, which has plenty of money but lacks access to weapons.
And the Houthis are not merely selling ordinary firearms to Al-Shabaab.
According to reports by The Wall Street Journal, attack drones have already made their way into Somalia, and there are concerns that missiles could follow.
The cooperation reportedly goes beyond simply transferring weapons.
The Houthis are also providing training.
Hundreds of Al-Shabaab fighters have reportedly traveled to Yemen to receive training in how to operate and use various weapons.
This is why the White House has identified cooperation between Al-Shabaab and the Houthis as one of the emerging threats it is monitoring.
Both groups regard their respective national governments as their primary enemies, but they also share a common enemy in the United States and Israel.
As the Houthis gain financial resources while Al-Shabaab gains access to attack drones, rockets, and potentially missiles, the Red Sea and Gulf of Aden are becoming increasingly dangerous.

One-line comment
The route from the Suez Canal through the Red Sea and into the Gulf of Aden is one of the world's most important global trade corridors.
With the Strait of Hormuz disrupted, Saudi Arabia has been using the Red Sea route to export crude oil.
Now that long-range strike weapons are potentially making their way into the hands of Al-Shabaab, which operates in the Gulf of Aden, the risks to both global shipping and oil supplies are increasing.
