The Yellowtail Season Is Just Around the Corner

Since it's the weekend, here's a little something about food.

Yellowtail migrate north in search of food from spring through summer, then head south from fall through winter to spend the colder months in warmer waters.

When they migrate as far south as Marado Island, off the southern tip of Jeju, they have built up a substantial layer of fat to prepare for winter. That's why people say that "winter yellowtail" tastes so good.

Yellowtail grow to about 5.6 kg at three years of age and 8.0 kg at four. This means that a fish weighing around 8 kg, commonly referred to as a large yellowtail (daebang-eo), is at least four years old.

Until the early 2010s, yellowtail weren't particularly highly regarded compared to today. Because of their high fat content, they were more often braised or grilled than served raw, and they were mainly consumed in Jeju.

Then came the tourism boom in Jeju during the 2010s. As more mainland Koreans visited the island and tried yellowtail, online reviews began appearing in greater numbers.

Korean consumers traditionally preferred fresh, chewy white-fleshed sashimi. However, tuna and salmon became popular first, gradually expanding people's tastes to include fatty, red-fleshed fish served after aging.

Yellowtail simply followed the path that tuna and salmon had paved.

These days, a lot of yellowtail are also caught along Korea's east coast, which is generally attributed to rising sea temperatures.

As coastal waters around Korea have become warmer overall, yellowtail that were once mainly caught around Jeju and the eastern coast of North Gyeongsang Province are now being caught as far north as Goseong in Gangwon Province.

However, most of the yellowtail caught along the east coast are small to medium-sized fish caught using set nets. They are essentially a different product from the large yellowtail served during the winter season.

In Japan, fishermen catch juvenile yellowtail, known as mojako, between April and July. These young fish live among floating masses of seaweed drifting in the ocean.

Because artificially hatched yellowtail fry can develop deformities, hatchery-raised fish account for less than 5% of Japan's farmed yellowtail production. Catching wild mojako remains the dominant method.

The mojako caught in spring are raised in offshore net pens and begin reaching the market the following autumn. As a result, if fewer mojako are caught in a given year, yellowtail prices tend to rise the following year.

There is an important difference between Korean and Japanese aquaculture. In Korea, the common practice is to catch relatively mature wild yellowtail and fatten them in captivity. In Japan, farmers catch juvenile fish and raise them for at least 18 months to two years.

Feed accounts for approximately 70% of the production cost of farmed yellowtail. A large portion of the feed consists of anchovies imported from South America.

When anchovy catches in South America decline, feed prices rise. This increases the production costs of Japanese farmed yellowtail and can ultimately affect the price of yellowtail served at sashimi restaurants in Korea.

According to Japanese market prices as of November 6, 2026, supplies of farmed yellowtail in offshore net pens were plentiful. The average market price was around 1,687 yen per kilogram, roughly the same as last year.

This suggests that imported Japanese farmed yellowtail will probably cost about the same this winter as it did last year.

Generally speaking, Japanese farmed yellowtail is the most expensive, Korean-farmed and fattened yellowtail comes next, and wild-caught Korean yellowtail is the least expensive.

So when a sashimi restaurant tells you that it serves Japanese farmed yellowtail, you can take that as a way of saying that they're using a relatively expensive product.

Farmed yellowtail are fed regularly and swim around less, so they tend to develop more fat, which gives them a different flavor. They also have a higher usable meat yield.

One of the main factors affecting the usable meat yield of yellowtail is parasites.

Yellowtail can carry two types of parasites: anisakis (the fish-borne roundworm commonly known as the whale worm in Korea) and yellowtail filarial worms.

Yellowtail filarial worms live inside the flesh, while anisakis are normally found in the internal organs. After the fish dies, anisakis can migrate from the organs into the flesh.

Yellowtail filarial worms are not harmful to humans, but they are a problem for sashimi restaurants, mainly because they look unpleasant.

These worms remain with their host fish throughout its life. The larger and older the yellowtail, the larger the parasites can become. They are therefore more commonly found in large yellowtail.

In other words, the older and larger the yellowtail, the more likely it is to contain larger filarial worms.

These worms cannot use humans as hosts, nor can they penetrate the stomach wall like anisakis. If accidentally swallowed, they are simply digested as protein.

When a restaurant finds one, it normally removes the affected area generously before serving the fish.

Yellowtail filarial worms are rarely found in Japanese farmed yellowtail.

This is because Japanese farmers catch the fish while they are still juveniles and raise them in net pens, feeding them only formulated feed and frozen raw feed.

The parasites enter fish through natural food sources such as plankton. Farmed fish are raised outside this natural food chain, which interrupts the parasites' life cycle.

Yellowtail filarial worms are therefore mainly found in wild fish and in fish that have been caught as adults and fattened in captivity.

These latter fish have spent several years swimming in the ocean, so they may already carry the parasites before being placed in net pens.

When filarial worms are found, the affected area must be removed generously. In severe cases, the entire fish may have to be discarded. This reduces the usable meat yield of both fattened and wild-caught yellowtail.

As for anisakis, their life cycle begins when their eggs are released into the ocean through the feces of whales and other marine mammals.

Small crustaceans such as krill consume the larvae, and fish such as mackerel and horse mackerel become infected when they eat these crustaceans.

Yellowtail then become infected when they eat these smaller fish.

Farmed yellowtail are less likely to become hosts for anisakis because their feed is frozen or heat-treated.

When anisakis enter the human body, they can penetrate the stomach lining and cause severe abdominal pain. They cannot be killed with ordinary deworming medication, so they generally have to be removed through an endoscopic procedure.

Anisakis usually migrate into the flesh only after the host fish dies. Therefore, if a live yellowtail is caught and its internal organs are removed promptly, this risk can largely be avoided.

One Final Thought

Yellowtail season is just around the corner.

Personally, I think small yellowtail caught along Korea's east coast are worth trying. They may be less fatty, but they have that distinctive, slightly firm yet tender texture characteristic of yellowtail, which makes them quite enjoyable to eat.

They're also considerably cheaper. And since they're younger fish, there's less concern about yellowtail filarial worms.

All things considered, I think they're excellent value for money.
