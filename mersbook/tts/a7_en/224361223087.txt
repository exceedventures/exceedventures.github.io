Title: Kumamoto Earthquake. Could an Even Bigger Earthquake Be Coming?

A major earthquake has struck Japan, so here's a summary of what happened.
At approximately 4:27 PM on July 28, 2026, a magnitude 7.1 earthquake with a depth of 10 km and a maximum Japanese seismic intensity of 7 struck Kumamoto in Kyushu, Japan.
Earthquakes are described using two different measurements: magnitude and intensity.
Magnitude is the total amount of energy released at the earthquake's source. It is similar to the wattage of a light bulb.
Just as a 100-watt light bulb is still 100 watts whether it is in the middle of a room or across the house, an earthquake's magnitude has only one value no matter where it is measured on Earth.
Intensity, on the other hand, measures how strongly the ground shakes and how much damage occurs at a specific location.
A 100-watt light bulb can be blinding when it is right in front of you but appear dim when viewed from another room. Likewise, while magnitude is a single fixed value, intensity varies depending on where it is measured.
Magnitude has no upper limit. Since modern seismographs were invented, the strongest earthquake ever recorded was the 1960 Chile earthquake, which measured magnitude 9.5.
Seismic intensity scales differ from country to country. Japan uses a 10-level intensity scale: 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, Lower 5, Upper 5, Lower 6, Upper 6, and 7.
Yesterday's Kumamoto earthquake reached Intensity 7, the highest level on Japan's scale.
At Intensity 7, crawling becomes nearly impossible, furniture is thrown around violently, and buildings without sufficient earthquake-resistant design face a high risk of collapse.
There has also been indirect damage. About one hour after the earthquake, a gas leak from earthquake-damaged equipment exploded at an AEON shopping mall, leaving around 20 employees unaccounted for.
Earth's outer shell, the tectonic plates, is constantly being created and destroyed.
Deep beneath the oceans, at mid-ocean ridges, hot magma rises and cools when it meets seawater, creating new oceanic crust.
As this newly formed crust pushes older crust outward, tectonic plates move slowly at a rate of a few centimeters per year.
After traveling thousands of kilometers, these plates eventually collide with other plates. When this happens, the heavier plate sinks beneath the lighter one.
This region, where one plate dives beneath another, is called a subduction zone.
Most of the world's largest earthquakes occur along these subduction zones.
The reason is that tectonic plates often do not slide smoothly. Instead, enormous friction causes them to become tightly locked together.
As stress continues to build, the upper plate eventually snaps upward, releasing an enormous amount of stored energy all at once.
That sudden release of energy produces earthquakes and, in many cases, tsunamis.
About 90% of the world's earthquakes occur along plate boundaries. Japan is considered one of the most dangerous places on Earth because four tectonic plates meet beneath the Japanese archipelago.
The Eurasian Plate, North American Plate, Pacific Plate, and Philippine Sea Plate are continuously colliding beneath Japan from different directions.
However, this Kumamoto earthquake was not the type of subduction-zone earthquake described above.
Instead, it was an active fault earthquake.
The earthquake occurred when the Hinagu Fault Zone, an active fault running through Kyushu, suddenly slipped.
In the Kumamoto region, the Philippine Sea Plate is moving beneath the Eurasian Plate at a rate of about 4 to 6 centimeters per year.
Over hundreds of years, this movement gradually increases stress along the fault.
Just as a balloon suddenly bursts after enough pressure builds up, the fault eventually reached its limit and suddenly slipped by several meters.
Kumamoto experienced another Intensity 7 earthquake ten years ago, in April 2016.
The epicenter of this earthquake was located about 20 kilometers south of the epicenter of the 2016 event.
If the 2016 earthquake relieved stress in the northern section of the Hinagu Fault, this earthquake likely released stress that had accumulated in the southern section.
This suggests that the current earthquake may not be the end of the sequence, and additional earthquakes remain a significant possibility.
After an Intensity 7 main shock, it is common for Intensity 5 to 6 aftershocks to continue for weeks or even months.
Buildings weakened by the main earthquake often collapse during these aftershocks.
Already, dozens of aftershocks, including one measuring magnitude 6.1, have continued to strike the Kumamoto area.
During the 2016 Kumamoto earthquake, the first major quake was initially believed to be the main shock.
However, two days later, an even larger earthquake struck, becoming the true main shock.
Experts are warning that people should remain alert and cautious for at least the next month.
Kumamoto is the heart of Japan's semiconductor industry.
Major companies located there include JASM, TSMC's manufacturing subsidiary in Japan, Sony, the world's leading manufacturer of image sensors, and Fujifilm, a major supplier of semiconductor materials.
These companies have evacuated employees and are inspecting their facilities, but ongoing aftershocks may delay the return to normal operations.
