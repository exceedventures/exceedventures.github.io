# How Did the “Glacial Tsunami” in Nepal Happen?

Here is a summary of the massive flooding caused by a glacier-related disaster in Nepal.

The recent flooding in Nepal has been described as a **“glacial tsunami.”** The scientific term commonly used for this type of event is **GLOF (Glacial Lake Outburst Flood)**, although the term “glacial tsunami” is being used because the resulting surge of water resembles a tsunami.

There are more than **5,000 glacial lakes** scattered throughout the high-altitude regions of the Himalayas, formed as glaciers melt.

As glaciers flow downhill, they push along soil, gravel, rocks, and other debris. These materials accumulate at the front of a glacier, forming a dam-like barrier. This mixture of soil, gravel, and rock debris is called a **moraine**, or glacial till. It can act as a natural dam, trapping meltwater and forming a lake.

The problem is that these natural moraine dams are not made of concrete. They consist of loose soil and rocks mixed with ice, making them relatively vulnerable to sudden impacts.

It is somewhat like having a giant water reservoir held back by a wall of sand.

There are two major ways such a natural dam can fail.

The first is through a **surge wave**.

A glacier avalanche, landslide, or large boulder can fall into a lake from the cliffs or slopes above it. When a large mass of debris crashes into the lake, it generates a powerful wave. If that wave overtops or destroys the moraine dam, the entire lake can suddenly rush downstream.

The second is when the **moraine dam itself collapses** because it can no longer withstand pressure caused by an earthquake, heavy rainfall, or other factors.

However, the recent disaster in Nepal appears to have started somewhat differently.

It began when a massive chunk of glacier collapsed and fell, generating an impact so powerful that it was reportedly detected by seismographs as an event equivalent to a **magnitude 5.2 earthquake**.

The ice, rocks, and soil contained in the collapsing glacier blocked the **Lhende Khola River** in the upper Tibetan region.

Water continued to accumulate behind the blockage created by the fallen glacier debris. Eventually, the natural dam burst and the accumulated water suddenly surged downstream.

In other words, **a glacial lake did not burst.** Instead, the glacier collapsed, the resulting mass of ice, rocks, and soil blocked the river and created a temporary natural dam, and the dam eventually failed as water accumulated behind it.

The mechanism was therefore similar to a GLOF in the sense that **water became temporarily trapped and was then suddenly released**, but the trapped water was not in a pre-existing glacial lake. It accumulated rapidly behind a newly formed blockage caused by the glacier collapse.

This is reportedly why Nepalese disaster authorities have avoided simply calling the event a GLOF and have instead used the somewhat ambiguous term **“glacier-inclusive flood.”**

There is also an important difference between an ordinary monsoon flood and a glacial flood.

There was **no rain in the affected area at the time**, and the area was relatively dry.

The water suddenly came rushing down in broad daylight, resembling a tsunami, leaving residents with almost no time to evacuate.

And it was not just water.

The flood carried enormous rocks, mud, and other debris, giving it tremendous destructive power. It could sweep away entire cars, buildings, bridges, and virtually everything else in its path.

The currently reported death toll stands at **362, with around 1,300 people reported missing**, although the number of casualties could continue to rise as search and rescue operations progress.

The area where this disaster occurred is also known to be prone to recurring GLOF events.

On **July 5, 2016**, a landslide occurred above the Gombathangsha Lake, sending debris into the lake and eventually causing the moraine dam to collapse.

On **July 8, 2025**, a glacial lake associated with the **Phurephu Glacier in Tibet** also burst, causing a similar type of flood.

The Nepalese government has not simply been sitting idle. It has also carried out preventive measures, including deliberately draining water from potentially dangerous glacial lakes.

For example, **Imja Lake**, located at an elevation of approximately **5,010 meters**, once experienced rapidly rising water levels and was considered a potential ticking time bomb.

The Nepalese Army and Sherpa workers spent six months excavating a drainage channel, releasing approximately **4 million cubic meters of water** and lowering the lake level by about **3.4 meters**.

Water has also been drained from **Tsho Rolpa**, one of Nepal's largest glacial lakes, with support from international organizations.

Dangerous glacial lakes are monitored and, when necessary, partially drained as part of risk-management efforts.

The problem in this case was that **the location that suddenly collapsed was not one of the lakes currently designated for intensive management.**

Another factor that increased the potential damage is the geography: **the upstream area is Tibet, which is under Chinese control, while the downstream area is Nepal.**

Nepal has **3,252 glaciers and 2,323 glacial lakes**, of which **21 are considered potentially dangerous glacial lakes**.

However, when Tibet and surrounding areas are included, the number of potentially dangerous glacial lakes rises to **47**. Of these, **25 are in Tibet, 21 are in Nepal, and one is in India**.

The three countries are interconnected because rivers originating in Tibet flow through Nepal and eventually into India.

Many of the locations capable of monitoring glacial lakes and detecting potential hazards are located in Tibet. However, there is reportedly **no system that can provide Nepal with real-time warnings** from those areas.

Tibet is also a politically extremely sensitive region under Chinese control, and China is reportedly reluctant to publicly release real-time data from the area.

One reason Korean nationals were affected by this disaster is that the region is a major hub for **Nepal's hydropower industry**.

The enormous elevation drop of rivers flowing down from the Himalayas makes the region ideal for hydropower generation. Since Nepal has very limited fossil-fuel resources, hydropower is essentially its main source of energy.

Hydropower plants have therefore been built along rivers fed by glacial meltwater.

In this flood, **14 hydropower plants were damaged**, affecting an estimated **20% of Nepal's electricity supply**.

There are now warnings of a **second potential flood** as well.

It is reportedly believed that the flow of the Lhende Khola River has been blocked in the Tibetan region about **18 kilometers upstream from the Rasuwagadhi border checkpoint**, creating a new lake.

According to China's Ministry of Water Resources, approximately **2 million cubic meters of water** had accumulated by Thursday. Another **1 million cubic meters** could accumulate over the next two to three days, potentially bringing the total to around **3 million cubic meters**.

Authorities are warning of a significant risk that the temporary dam could burst within three days, potentially causing another major flood downstream.

This second flood warning is particularly notable because **China is now warning Nepal about the potential danger.**

### One-line comment

Apparently, even though there was no rain and it was broad daylight, a wall of water reportedly rose to as much as **9 meters within just 30 minutes** and came rushing downstream.

It seems that anywhere with a valley can be vulnerable to this kind of disaster—the scale may differ, but the underlying danger is much the same.

The **Upper Trishuli-1 Hydropower Project**, which began construction in 2022 and is scheduled for completion by the end of this year, is expected to supply around **10% of Nepal's total electricity** and is the country's largest hydropower project. More than **90% of the intake weir**, which blocks the river and raises the water level for water intake, was reportedly destroyed.

The project was being constructed by **Korea South-East Power (KOEN)** and **Doosan Enerbility**. Nine Korean employees—three from KOEN, five from Doosan Enerbility, and one locally hired Korean employee—are currently reported missing.

All we can do is hope that **all nine return home safely**.

It is striking to think about how Korean companies and workers are quietly carrying out major projects and contributing in so many places around the world.
