Title: The Secret Behind China’s Growing Share of the Seaweed Market

Here’s a light topic for a day off.
Seaweed is commercially farmed on a large scale mainly in Korea, China, and Japan.
Southeast Asian countries such as Thailand and Indonesia cannot farm it on a large scale because seaweed needs seawater temperatures to fall below 20°C before it can really start growing. That makes cultivation difficult in warm Southeast Asian waters.
Even in Korea, seaweed cannot be farmed during the summer when seawater temperatures are high. Instead, it is cultivated from around November through May, when the water is cold enough, and the harvest is enough to supply the country for the rest of the year.
Korea’s main variety is Porphyra yezoensis, commonly known in Korea as bangsamuneuigim. It is thin and soft, making it easy to process, and is usually sold as seasoned roasted seaweed.
Japan’s main variety is also the same type of seaweed, but the seasoning method is different from Korea’s.
In Japan, roasted seaweed is typically seasoned with soy sauce and sugar, giving it a slightly sweet flavor.
In Korea, seaweed is seasoned with oil and salt, and the Korean-style version has become increasingly popular.
China’s main seaweed variety is Pyropia haitanensis, known as dan-gim in Korea.
One advantage of this variety is that it can be cultivated even in relatively warm water. However, its texture is tougher, making it unsuitable for seasoned roasted seaweed. Instead, it is mainly used for making soups and broths.
More than 80% of the seaweed cultivated in China is this variety. However, in Jiangsu Province, north of the Yangtze River where seawater temperatures are lower, the Korean-style bangsamuneuigim variety is also cultivated.
The problem is pollution.
China’s coastal waters are severely polluted, with some areas becoming so contaminated that they are essentially dead zones where marine life cannot survive.
According to China’s marine environmental surveys, when 600 locations along the Chinese coast were sampled, as many as 84% of the sites showed pollutant concentrations above permissible standards.
Coastal water quality is poor from the northern Bohai Bay—including the Liaodong and Bohai regions—to the Yangtze River estuary and the southern coast.
The coastal waters of Jiangsu Province also suffer from relatively serious heavy-metal contamination, including cadmium.
And this is particularly important for seaweed because seaweed absorbs the seawater in which it grows. In other words, water quality directly affects the quality of the seaweed.
Seaweed grown in China is largely consumed domestically, but when it comes to exporting it to markets such as the United States, there are problems not only with taste but also with quality.
This is one of the reasons China, despite being the world’s largest seaweed producer, is buying up large quantities of dried seaweed from Korea.
Exports of dried Korean seaweed to China jumped from 2,543 tons in 2024 to 4,539 tons in 2025, and another 2,416 tons were exported in just the first half of this year.
Dried seaweed sells for around 8,500 won per pack of 100 sheets, while seasoned roasted seaweed can sell for around 25,000 won.
Chinese companies import dried Korean seaweed, season it Korean-style with oil and salt, and then export it using Korean-language branding that can make it look like a Korean product.
Because they use Korean dried seaweed, the taste is almost indistinguishable from Korean-made seasoned seaweed. Yet the price is significantly lower.
That is why Chinese seasoned seaweed is steadily increasing its market share.
This is also one reason why seasoned seaweed—the more profitable category—has fallen from 43% of Korea’s total seaweed export volume in 2020 to 35% in 2025.
Korea’s current premium seaweed variety is Ipbadi-dolgim, commonly called gopchang-gim.
The name gopchang-gim literally comes from its appearance. When freshly harvested, the raw seaweed is curly and twisted, resembling pork intestines (gopchang). Its official name is Ipbadi-dolgim.
It is produced for only about 20 days a year, from late October to mid-November.
Because of its excellent flavor and limited production, it can sell for three to five times the price of ordinary seaweed.
There is also an even rarer variety called Cham-gim, which is considered the original type of seaweed traditionally used for gimbap.
Compared with bangsamuneuigim, Cham-gim contains more umami amino acids, protein, and minerals, and its flavor is considered significantly superior.
However, it has a low yield and is vulnerable to disease, so it was gradually replaced by bangsamuneuigim and has nearly disappeared.
So if you sometimes feel that gimbap doesn't taste as good as it used to, you may simply be remembering the flavor of gimbap made with Cham-gim.
There is also a new opportunity emerging for Korean seaweed exports to the United States.
The reason is that U.S. rice inventories have increased significantly.
The U.S. has been looking for ways to deal with its excess rice inventories and has started paying attention to Korean seaweed as a product that could help promote rice consumption.
Dried seaweed has characteristics similar to an agricultural or fishery commodity.
The U.S. takes a very conservative approach to imported raw seafood and other products that could compete with American farmers and fishermen.
Seasoned roasted seaweed, however, is different.
Unlike dried seaweed, roasted and seasoned seaweed can be classified as a processed food rather than raw seaweed.
The U.S. tends to be more open to processed consumer foods that are not produced domestically and therefore do not compete directly with American farmers or fishermen, while providing benefits to consumers.
Seasoned seaweed fits this category perfectly.
As the popularity of gimbap continues to spread, Americans are also increasingly eating seasoned seaweed together with rice.
So seasoned seaweed is something of a win-win product: it can help the U.S. deal with excess rice inventories, does not significantly harm American farmers or fishermen, and is popular with consumers.
That is why the U.S. has kept the 15% tariff on Korean dried seaweed, while cutting the tariff on seasoned roasted seaweed from 15% to 0%.

One-line takeaway
Gopchang-gim is delicious when eaten with sesame oil and salt, while gimbap tastes best when wrapped in Cham-gim. For ordinary seasoned seaweed, Sambujain seems like a safe choice. And the uni gimbap at Yeongdo Haenyeochon is also delicious.
